A personal glossary is one of the most underrated tools in a knowledge wiki. As you explore new subjects, you inevitably encounter unfamiliar jargon, borrowed concepts, and domain-specific shorthand. Instead of letting those terms blur together over time, capturing them here in plain language gives you a reliable reference you can revisit and refine. Think of this page as a living document — one that grows alongside your learning and reflects the way you actually understand things, not just how a textbook defines them.

## How to Use the Glossary

<Note>
  Terms in this glossary are organized alphabetically. Where a term connects to a dedicated topic page elsewhere in the wiki, you'll find a link in its **See Also** field. If you're looking for something specific, use your browser's find function (`Cmd+F` / `Ctrl+F`) to jump straight to it.
</Note>

Browse the glossary when you encounter a term you've seen before but can't quite place, or use it as a starting point when exploring a new area. Update definitions whenever your understanding deepens — a definition that made sense six months ago might deserve a more precise rewrite today.

## Glossary Entry Format

Each entry follows a consistent structure so you can scan and compare terms quickly. Use this template when adding new entries:

```md
## [Term]
**Category:** Technology / Science / Productivity / etc.
**Definition:** Clear, plain-language definition.
**See Also:** Related terms or pages.
**Source:** Where you encountered this term.
```

Keep the **Definition** field to two or three sentences at most. If a concept deserves more depth, write a dedicated topic note and link to it from the **See Also** field rather than expanding the glossary entry itself.

## Sample Entries

### Zettelkasten

The Zettelkasten (German for "slip-box") is a note-taking method developed by sociologist Niklas Luhmann, built around the idea of atomic, interlinked notes. Each note captures a single idea and connects explicitly to related notes, forming a web rather than a hierarchy. Over time, the system surfaces unexpected connections and generates insights that no single note could produce on its own.

### Second Brain

A Second Brain is a personal knowledge management system — an external, digital store for ideas, notes, research, and resources that you want to remember and act on. The concept, popularized by Tiago Forte, offloads the burden of remembering everything from your biological memory to a trusted, searchable system. A well-maintained Second Brain lets you build on past thinking instead of starting from scratch each time.

### Spaced Repetition

Spaced repetition is a learning technique that schedules review sessions at strategically increasing intervals — revisiting material just before you're likely to forget it. Rooted in the spacing effect discovered by Hermann Ebbinghaus, it dramatically improves long-term retention compared to massed practice (cramming). Tools like Anki implement spaced repetition algorithmically, adjusting intervals based on how well you recall each item.

### Deep Work

Deep work, a term coined by Cal Newport, refers to focused, distraction-free effort on cognitively demanding tasks that push your abilities to their limit. It stands in contrast to shallow work — logistical, low-value tasks that can be performed while distracted. Cultivating the ability to do deep work consistently is, according to Newport, one of the most valuable and increasingly rare skills in the modern economy.

### Mental Model

A mental model is a simplified internal representation of how something works — a cognitive framework you use to understand, predict, and navigate the world. Good mental models compress complex systems into actionable principles; poor ones lead to blind spots and flawed decisions. Building a broad library of mental models drawn from disciplines like physics, economics, and psychology improves the quality of your thinking across domains.

<Tip>
  Keep each definition short enough to read in under thirty seconds. If you find yourself writing a paragraph, that's a sign the term deserves its own dedicated note — link to it instead and keep the glossary entry as a concise pointer.
</Tip>
